Graduating from Modernity and the Suzukan Seminar

Sotsu-kindai (卒近代) — a learning community that trains social producers and epoch makers

日本語 · English · 简体中文 · 繁體中文

Graduating from Modernity (Sotsu-kindai, 卒近代) is an idea born in Japan. Instead of rejecting modernity, we graduate from it with gratitude for what it gave us — and build a new layer centred on life and relationships on top of it. The idea was coined around 1995 by Kan Suzuki (known as "Suzukan"), who in the same year began the Suzukan Seminar (Kan Suzuki Lab): a community of people who aspire to become social producers for graduating from modernity, where peers learn and grow together. Modelled on Yoshida Shoin's school Shoka Sonjuku, it has continued across Keio University SFC, the University of Tokyo and ZEN University, and about 1,000 people have taken part over some 30 years.

The idea — Graduating from Modernity

What is modernity?

The modernity in question began in Britain, France and the United States in the late eighteenth century, reached Japan with the Meiji Restoration, and spread across Asia and Latin America after the Second World War. Its hallmarks are rich-nation-strong-army policies, material and mechanical civilisation, GDP supremacy, mass production, mass consumption and mass disposal, and competition and efficiency — along with a way of seeing that breaks the world into simple parts (reductionism) and measures people by function and ability.

Modernity brought immense benefits: prosperity, science, freedom. Yet in the twentieth century its premises were shaken from within. Philosophy questioned the supremacy of reason; natural science, the deterministic worldview; social science, the rational, calculating homo economicus; brain science, rational judgement and free will. In the twenty-first century these tremors surfaced as events — 9/11, the global environmental crisis, the Great East Japan Earthquake, a global pandemic. Kan Suzuki's Declaration of Graduating from Modernity (2025) sees excessive growth-first modernisation at the root of today's unrest.

"Graduating", not "post" — how it differs from postmodernism

Many voices have criticised modernity before. But the Declaration observes that because they only negated modernity, they never won broad support — and could not escape modernity's spell. Negation breeds further negation. To break this chain, it proposes non-negation.

Think of school: dropping out is "de-" (脱, datsu); learning what you came to learn and then moving on is "graduating" (卒, sotsu). Postmodern thought — rendered in Japanese as "de-modern" (脱近代) — set out to doubt and dismantle modernity's "grand narratives". Graduating from modernity inherits those questions but does not stop at critique. Nor does it discard modernity and replace it: it layers something new on top of the best of the modern and the pre-modern (superposition). As Kan Suzuki puts it, graduating from modernity is not the name of an era but an attitude.

Why graduate from modernity?

One reason is that modernity's greatest driver has not only been big capital or big parties, but all of us who believed in it and supported it. Rather than defeating an external enemy, each of us must change, from the inside, values that have stuck to us — such as judging people and life choices by economic gain alone.

So graduating from modernity chooses play and learning over struggle: singing and playing music together, enjoying sport, theatre and art, and talking about them. People's values only truly change through things they are wholeheartedly absorbed in. Like the judo maxim "softness controls hardness", the aim is not to topple modernity but to master it.

Nor is it a prediction of a fixed future. It is an act of will: each person thinking about what to keep from modernity, what to stop, and what to recover from before it — deliberating with others and expressing it in their own way. When people who have slightly changed how they spend their time and what they value become more than half of society, the balance of the world shifts.

The measure of value becomes well-being — of people, society and the planet. Other key words are co-creation through resonance (響創, in place of competition) and the revolution through play and learning (遊学革命). To explore the books behind these ideas, start with Beyond Modernity: 330 Books from a Japanese University Seminar.

Who the seminar trains — social producers and epoch makers

The Suzukan Seminar aims to nurture social producers: people who connect with many others, gather and combine social resources, and create and deliver social value that no one could create alone. This work is called social production.

Beyond that lies the social orchestrator — someone who, like the conductor of an orchestra, draws out the best in diverse people and brings them into harmony, and who keeps facing society's hard problems. Caught between competing values and interests (dilemmas), they take them on as their own, relying on a grounding in history and philosophy to judge.

Epoch makers — people who make an era

The seminar's other key word is epoch maker. Just as "epoch-making" means marking the start of a new era, an epoch maker is someone who creates a new era — who finds new forms of happiness for individuals, society and the planet, notices them, creates them, deepens them and spreads them. If "social producer" describes how one acts, "epoch maker" describes what one brings about: diving early into fields no one is watching yet, connecting people and resources, and making happen the events later remembered as turning points.

Behind this is the conviction that "it is people, not the economy, that move the world". While working in Yamaguchi Prefecture, Kan Suzuki visited Shoka Sonjuku many times and was struck by how young people who studied there for only a few years in a small room went on to move Japan. That is how the seminar began.

The method — PCCP × AAR

The seminar's common language is PCCP. Whatever field members study, they share the same way of building projects.

LayerWhat it is
PhilosophyThe values that anchor every decision. Unchanging as long as the actor exists (though it deepens). If it changes, the project is over.
ConceptThe angle that matters most in this time and place. It changes from stage to stage.
ContentsThe concrete activities that embody the philosophy — projects with proper names.
ProgramThe 5W2H needed to start an activity. If even one is missing, nothing starts.

PCCP is positioned as a replacement for PDCA, which relies on the plan as the basis for judgement ("graduating from PDCA"). When premises keep changing, philosophy and concept — together with clear dos and don'ts — are put into words first, instead of a plan. Execution runs on the AAR cycle: Anticipation, Action, Reflection.

There is also a pattern from finding a problem to acting on it: decide whose happiness you want to create; examine the problem with both data and episodes; map the stakeholders and the causal links between issues; untangle structures such as trade-offs and dilemmas; then gather people and resources and act. Underneath lies training in public philosophy — knowing which philosophy you stand on when you judge.

How people learn

Dialogue — "beat the drum"

Following Shoka Sonjuku, the seminar values one-to-one dialogue and deliberation over lecturing to a class. The founder sits at the back and responds when students raise questions — like a drum that only sounds when struck, learning starts with the students. Planning sessions and running the seminar are handled by a student executive team.

"Go on a journey"

First-hand experience gained with one's own body, and encounters with the unknown, are prized. Journeys include travelling to the field — and also meeting great books, great performances and great masters.

Projects

Members first join projects prepared by the seminar with their peers, and once they have experience, launch their own. Retreats and stays in local communities take learning outside the classroom.

FAQ

What is Graduating from Modernity (Sotsu-kindai)?

An idea born in Japan and coined by Kan Suzuki around 1995. Instead of rejecting modernity, we graduate from it with gratitude for what it gave us, and build a new layer centred on life and relationships on top of the modern and the pre-modern.

How is it different from postmodernism?

Postmodernism doubts and dismantles modernity — in Japanese it is translated as "de-modern" (脱近代). Graduating from modernity does not reject it: it chooses what to keep, what to stop and what to recover from the pre-modern, and layers something new on top. It is an attitude rather than the name of an era, and it is carried out as practice in society (social production), not only as critique.

What is a social producer?

A person who connects with many people, gathers and combines social resources, and creates and delivers social value that no one could create alone — the kind of person the Suzukan Seminar aims to nurture.

What is an epoch maker?

"Epoch-making" means opening a new era; an epoch maker is a person who creates one — who finds, creates, deepens and spreads new forms of happiness for individuals, society and the planet.

What is PCCP?

Philosophy, Concept, Contents, Program: a four-layer framework for structuring projects and life decisions, put into action through the AAR cycle (Anticipation, Action, Reflection).
